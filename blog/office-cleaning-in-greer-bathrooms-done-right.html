What a commercial restroom needs that a home bathroom does not

Traffic is the difference. A home bathroom is used by four people; an office restroom might be used by thirty. Dispensers run out, trash fills before the next visit and floors take a beating around the toilets and sinks. Commercial restrooms also carry a hygiene expectation that goes beyond looks, so we disinfect, and we keep the contact time on the label instead of wiping dry right away.

The high-touch points people forget

  • Door pull and push plate, inside and out
  • Stall latches and coat hooks
  • Flush handles or sensor buttons
  • Faucet handles and soap dispenser buttons
  • Paper towel dispenser levers
  • Light switch and baby changing station, if there is one
  • Trash can lids and lids of sanitary bins

These are the surfaces that carry the most contact, and a quick spray and wipe of the counter leaves them untouched. We clean them every visit.

A typical pass through one restroom

We bring a separate color of cloth for restrooms and never reuse them elsewhere in the office. The crew empties the trash first, restocks paper, soap and sanitary supplies if your team keeps stock on site, and then sprays the fixtures. While the product sits, we do mirrors, counters and sinks, and then return to the toilets and urinals with a brush. The floor is last: swept, then mopped with fresh solution, edges and the area behind the toilet included. A restroom floor that is only mopped down the middle starts to smell within days.

Keeping it consistent

Consistency beats intensity. A restroom cleaned weekly will usually need a heavier pass than one cleaned every night. If your staff is large, ask about extra restocking days. If budget is tight, a lighter weekly visit with a monthly deeper pass often works well. We will tell you honestly what the space needs rather than selling a larger plan.

What the first week looks like

When a Greer office starts with us, the first visit is the longest. We note the restroom supplies, the type of soap dispensers, the floor drains and the spots that smell. The second visit is shorter because the base layer of soil is gone. By the third week you can usually tell if the schedule is right: if the trash is overflowing, add a visit; if the floors still look fresh on cleaning day, you might be able to stretch it. We ask for your feedback after the first week and adjust.

One more detail that matters in small offices is odor control. A restroom that smells is almost always a floor or drain problem, not a toilet problem, which is why we mop the edges and the area behind the fixtures.
