Three kinds of space, three kinds of scope

A building has more than one cleaning job inside it. The lobby and corridors need a steady routine, an occupied suite follows the tenant's own schedule, and a vacant suite needs a full reset before the next lease. If you manage commercial space in Greer, splitting the work this way makes office cleaning in Greer easier to price, easier to inspect and easier to explain to tenants.

Common areas: the routine that tenants notice

Tenants rarely compliment the cleaning but they complain fast when it slips. The things people see are the entry glass, the floor inside the door, restrooms, trash and the break room if shared. A routine visit includes:

  • High-touch points: handles, switches and keypads
  • Restrooms cleaned, disinfected and restocked
  • Glass doors and entry areas
  • Reception and waiting areas dusted
  • Trash and recycling removed
  • Floors vacuumed and mopped, with different care for carpet, tile and hard surfaces

Ask any vendor, us included, to put these items and their days in writing. A short schedule on paper prevents most disagreements.

Vacant suites between leases

An empty suite needs more than a quick vacuum. Window sills and tracks, closets, vents, baseboards and the inside of any kitchenette get attention before photos and showings. If the previous tenant renovated, post-construction cleaning may be the right job. Taking pictures after the clean gives you a clear before-and-after record for the next tenant.

When we walk a building for the first time, the first thing we check is how tenants move through it. The path from the parking lot to the front door, the restroom nearest the entry and the corridor outside the busiest suite get the most traffic and show wear first. That tells us where a few extra minutes make the building look maintained all week. A property with a well-kept entry and clean restrooms gives the impression that everything else is looked after.

Floors deserve their own plan. Carpet in corridors, tile in restrooms and hard floors in the lobby each need a different method, and using the wrong one is how floors get dull or damaged. Tell us what is installed, and which areas get the most red clay and rain tracked in during wet weeks.

Tell us about quiet hours, loading-dock rules and any tenant who needs their suite left alone on certain days. Those small rules, agreed on paper before the first visit, are what separate a smooth start from a month of corrections.

Communication keeps a cleaning contract healthy. Agree on one contact for the property, a way to report a miss (a text or call is usually fastest) and a response time. When a tenant complains, you want to forward the message, not investigate. We recommend a short monthly check-in at first, then less often once the routine is stable.

It also pays to think about seasons. Spring pollen leaves a yellow film on entry glass, summer humidity makes restrooms need more attention and winter brings salt and mud onto the lobby floor. Adjusting the visit list a little through the year keeps the building looking consistent.

How long it takes and how soon we can start

Common areas in a small building are often a one- or two-hour job per visit. A vacant suite can take longer if it needs a reset. Greer sits along I-85 and US-29, so tenants and vendors reach the property easily, and we are normally able to talk about starting within the week after the walkthrough.

For buildings with several tenants, one clear sign helps: a notice in the lobby with the cleaning days and a phone number for reports. Tenants stop emailing five different people and the feedback reaches the right place. Managers also like to keep the scope for common areas separate from suites, so that when one tenant asks for extra service, it does not blur the contract for everybody else.

We are happy to walk a building at a quiet hour, such as early morning or evening, so we see it the way the crew will. That is the best time to spot problems like a worn mat at the entry or a restroom exhaust fan that does not run.
