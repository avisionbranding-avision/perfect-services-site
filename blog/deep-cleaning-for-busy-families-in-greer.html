The kitchen, where the family lives

Ask a Greer parent which room needs the most help and it is almost always the kitchen. Cabinet fronts around the stove carry a film of cooking grease that wipes smooth with a damp cloth but comes back after one weekend of pancakes. The handles at child height collect fingerprints. The range hood and the backsplash take the splatter from every weeknight dinner. Our team degreases these and works top to bottom, so crumbs fall to the floor and the floor gets done last.

The inside of the oven and fridge are optional add-ons, and they are popular before holiday cooking. Mention them at the estimate and we plan the time for them.

Bedrooms, playrooms and bathrooms

Kids' rooms are the part of the house where clutter decides how deep the clean can go. We clean what is clear. If the floor is covered with toys, we work around them and dust the shelves and sills, then vacuum what we can reach. A fifteen-minute tidy the night before lets the team do the baseboards, under the bed edges and the corners of closets. In the playroom, a damp microfiber cloth beats a dry duster, since dry dusting just lifts particles into the air.

In bathrooms, grout lines and hard water spots are the slowest work and the biggest difference. We scrub the grout in the shower and around the tub, clean behind and around the toilet base and wipe down the vanity, mirror and chrome. Children's bathrooms often have toothpaste on the faucet and a step stool that has not been moved in months.

Before the team arrives: a short family checklist

  • Pick up toys, clothes and papers from floors and counters.
  • Put loose school items, homework and backpacks in one spot.
  • Tell us about any room that is off limits, such as a napping child's bedroom.
  • Decide on add-ons: oven, fridge, cabinets or blinds.
  • Let us know about allergies. Dust removed instead of moved matters for kids with allergies or asthma.
  • Plan for the day to be a bit disruptive. A deep clean takes longer than a regular visit.

Parents often ask whether to be home. You can, and some like to be, but it is not necessary. Many families leave a code or hide-a-key arrangement and come home to a finished house. Either way, we do a closing walkthrough of each room and leave notes about anything we noticed, such as a cracked caulk line in the shower or a loose handle on a cabinet.

Greer's pollen season and humid summers mean spring and early fall are common times for families to book. A deep clean before the school year starts, or before holiday guests, gets the house to a clean baseline.

Windows, blinds and the sliding door

Window sills and tracks catch pollen in spring and condensation in humid summers. We clean the sills, tracks and blinds during a deep clean, and the sliding-door track is the one that surprises families most, because it fills with crumbs, sand and dead insects. A small brush and a damp cloth is how we do it, and it takes more time than most people expect.

After the deep clean: keeping it going

Most families who book a deep cleaning follow it with regular visits. The first visit sets the standard, and a weekly or biweekly house cleaning keeps it there. This is the order we recommend for homes that have not had professional help in months: deep cleaning first, then a recurring plan. If you are not ready for regular help, repeat the deep clean once or twice a year, and tidy in between.
