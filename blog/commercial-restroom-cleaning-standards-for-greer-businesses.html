What a clean commercial restroom looks like

A restroom that passes inspection has no streaks on the mirror, no film on the faucet, and no smell at the door. The floor is clean along the wall and around the base of the toilet, where mop heads rarely reach. Dispensers are full and wiped on the outside. Trash is out and the liner is fresh.

What the team usually finds when they take over a restroom is not dirt on the obvious surfaces. It is buildup in the corners, on the back of the toilet seat hinge, under the sink lip and on the edge of the door where hands push. Those are the spots that tell you whether someone cleaned or only sprayed.

The order we work in

Order keeps the work clean. Skipping around means touching a disinfected surface with a dirty cloth.

  1. Set up supplies and put on gloves; separate cloths are used for restrooms only.
  2. Remove trash and replace liners.
  3. Apply disinfectant to toilets and urinals and let it sit while the rest is done.
  4. Clean mirrors, then counters and sinks, then faucets and handles.
  5. Wipe dispensers, partitions, door handles and light switches.
  6. Scrub the toilet and urinal, including the base and behind the seat.
  7. Restock soap, towels and tissue.
  8. Sweep and mop the floor from the farthest corner out to the door.

Dwell time is the part owners most often skip when they clean in a rush. Disinfectant needs to stay wet on a surface to do its job, which is why the toilets are treated first.

Matching restroom care to your kind of business

A small office on one floor near the Trade Street area has a different need from a salon with a client sink or a retail store drawing traffic off US-29. Fewer users means less wear, but odors and floor buildup still show up in tight, closed rooms. Higher traffic means restroom cleaning should not wait until the end of the day.

Ask for a short scope in writing. List what is cleaned each visit, what is cleaned weekly, such as walls and vents, and who restocks. A clear scope is the easiest way to avoid a gap between what you expect and what shows up.

If you also need the break room and entry cleaned on the same visit, that is part of the standard commercial and office cleaning service. If your staff handles kitchen items, mention it so the plan reflects that.

Problems a good restroom routine prevents

Most complaints about business restrooms come from three things: odor, empty dispensers and sticky floors. Odor usually traces back to the floor edge and the base of the toilet, not the bowl. Empty dispensers are a restocking problem, which is why restocking is part of every visit. Sticky floors happen when a mop is pushed through dirty water, so the water is changed between rooms and the floor is cleaned last.

Seasonal wear matters too. Humid Upstate summers keep moisture in small restrooms longer, and a restroom with no window needs more attention to the ceiling vent and the grout. If your own staff wipes things down between our visits, agree on a short list so nothing gets done twice or missed.

Owners ask whether restroom care should be separate from the rest of the office clean. It does not need to be: the same visit covers both, and office and restroom cleaning in Greer is planned around your hours and your traffic.

How to inspect the work yourself

Do a ten-second check each morning. Open the door and note the smell. Look at the base of the toilet and the floor in the corner. Touch the faucet to feel for film. Check the dispensers. If something is off, tell the team directly; a good cleaner would rather hear it the same week than have you stay quiet.
