Start with the lease, not the vacuum

If you rent a suite, a storefront or a small studio in Greer, the first question is who is responsible for cleaning. Many commercial leases put interior cleaning on the tenant and common areas on the landlord. Some do the reverse. Read that clause, then decide whether you want a regular office cleaning service in Greer or a one-time clean for a lease end or a new move-in.

What a renter's office usually needs

Small offices, salons, studios and retail spaces share a pattern: heavy contact on a handful of surfaces and a lot of foot traffic at the entrance. A routine visit covers:

  • Desks, counters and workstations wiped
  • Handles, switches and keypads, the high-touch points
  • Restrooms cleaned, disinfected and restocked
  • Break room sink, microwave and refrigerator exterior
  • Trash and recycling removed
  • Floors vacuumed and mopped
  • Glass doors and entry areas
  • Reception and waiting areas dusted

The entry door glass and the floor just inside it are what clients see first, so those get attention at every visit.

These are starting points, not rules. We look at your space and your hours at the visit, and many offices prefer after-hours cleaning so the team never gets in the way. Cleaning when the building is closed also means the keys, alarm and access details are agreed in advance.

Things renters often forget

Carpet near the entry holds Greer's red clay longer than people expect, and tile restrooms need a different approach from carpeted offices. Break-room refrigerators and microwaves become the part nobody owns, so write down who handles them. If you are leaving, keep in mind that a landlord's inspection of a commercial suite also reaches window sills, vents and the insides of closets.

Many small businesses start cleaning themselves, and for a while that works. The problem shows up when the owner is the one wiping the restroom at six o'clock after a long day, or when a client mentions the smell in the waiting room. At that point the question is no longer cost against free but your time against a routine that happens whether or not you remember. A crew arrives on schedule, follows the same list and leaves the space ready for the next morning.

Think about supplies too. Some tenants buy their own paper goods and soap; others ask the cleaners to restock. Decide in advance, and tell the team where supplies are kept. It sounds minor, but missing paper towels are the most common complaint in shared restrooms.

Be honest about the quirks of your space during the visit. Salons have hair and chemical residue near the chairs, studios have mirrors that show every smear, and clinics have waiting rooms with soft seating. Retail shops have glass cases and display shelves that need careful hands. Sharing these details lets the team bring the right cloths and plan the route through the room.

Finally, if you are close to the end of your lease, ask about a one-time reset: baseboards, vents, window sills and the inside of the break-room fridge, which are the spots a commercial landlord checks at move-out. Doing it once, thoroughly, beats a weekend of scrubbing.

How long it takes

A small suite is often a one- or two-hour visit. Larger layouts or several restrooms take longer. Our location is close, about a ten-minute drive from most of Greer along SC-14, SC-101 or US-29, so scheduling a mid-week slot is generally workable.

If you share a building, ask which areas the landlord already covers so you do not pay twice. Hallways, the shared restroom and the parking-lot door are common examples. Write the boundary down in an email, since it saves arguments when a new manager takes over. It also tells us exactly where our work begins and ends, which keeps the visit efficient and the invoice clear.

Alarm and key handling is the last practical piece. A short note on how the team enters, which door to lock and whom to call if the alarm trips covers most of what can go wrong on a first visit.
